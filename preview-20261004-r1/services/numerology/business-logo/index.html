Business logo guidance

A logo is a picture, and numerology reads numbers. The two meet because a mark has things that can be counted: the letters in its wordmark, the initials it uses, the parts it is built from, the colours it carries. Practitioners compare those counts with the name’s number and with the owner’s own, and take colours from the planet that each number belongs to.

These pages explain how a number-based interpretation might be discussed beside a designer's decisions. They do not promise a designer brief, mark review or logo production from Narayani.

Questions about a business mark

  • The name’s total and your own numbers, which every other point is read against
  • What the mark counts: letters, parts, colours, and any number that keeps recurring
  • The colour families the lists name for those numbers, and how far the lists agree
  • What your designer has already proposed, and which choices already agree with the numbers
  • What a stranger should take from the mark in its first two seconds

Where numbers fit in a logo, and where they do not

Numbers can offer reasons for a few choices: how many parts a mark has, which colour families to try, whether its lines rise or rest. They cannot say whether a mark stays legible at the size of a phone icon, prints well in one colour or will be remembered by a stranger. Those questions belong to the designer, and where they conflict with a number, they win.

A name total, initials and a birth date are different inputs. The educational examples below keep them visible so that you can compare calculations. They do not establish Narayani's preferred table or review protocol.

What is checked in a mark

Each point below is a count or a convention, and each is read against the name’s number.

Explore what is checked
The name’s number
The sum of the letters in the name customers actually use, worked out in a named table, with the compound number kept alongside. Every other point is read against this one. The business name page shows how a total is made and why it varies.
Initials
If the mark uses initials, they are counted as a small name of their own, and some practitioners read that total beside the full name’s. Whether a mark should carry initials at all is a design question, and the designer’s to answer.
Letter count
The number of letters in the wordmark, read as a number and reduced: ten letters give 1. Capitals, spacing and typeface do not change it. A change of spelling does.
Number of parts
The count of separate parts a mark is built from: petals, bars, stripes, dots, windows. This is the most direct way a number enters a logo, because a count can be chosen. A mark with five leaves carries a 5.
Shapes
What can be counted in a shape is its corners, points or segments, and what can be observed is which way it points. A reading takes those counts like any other number, as an option for the designer and never as a rule.
Colours
Colour families come from lists that tie each number to a planet, set out in the table below. The number of colours in a mark is also a count. A colour that fails on a screen or in print is no use to anyone, whatever list it came from.
Direction and weight of type
Direction is whether the lines and arrows of a mark rise, rest or fall. In signature reading, which has its own page, some lists prefer a slight upward slant and others give different advice. Weight is whether the lettering is light, regular or bold, which is mainly a design matter. Neither is a rule, and the designer judges both.
Repeated numbers
A number that recurs in a mark: a name of eight letters, a mark with eight parts, an owner whose Mulank is 8. In Lo Shu grid reading, which has its own page, a repeated digit is read as emphasis, and the thresholds vary from list to list. Whether emphasis is wanted is for the owner to say.

Colour, and the planets behind it

Indian usage ties each number to a planet and counts Rahu and Ketu, the Moon’s two nodes, among the planets. Published lists tie each planet to families of colour, and the table shows the families they commonly name. The lists differ from one another, and for Rahu and Ketu they vary too widely to name a family at all.

A brief for an invented business

Take an invented business, Tanvi Looms, a handloom shop. Its name has ten letters and totals 5 in both the Chaldean and the Pythagorean table. Its owner was born on 8 November 1991, which gives a Mulank of 8 and a Bhagyank of 3. Here is how those numbers might become guidance.

What outranks the numbers

Guidance of this kind goes to a designer, so it helps to be plain about who has the last word. These points come first, whatever a total suggests.

Explore what comes first
  • Legibility. The mark has to read at thumbnail size and on a shop board.
  • Reproduction. It has to work in one colour, in black on white, stitched or embossed, wherever you will use it.
  • Contrast. Some people see colour differently, and a mark that depends on two colours few can tell apart will fail them.
  • Distinctiveness. A mark close to another business’s may confuse customers and can lead to a dispute. Only a trade mark search can show how close, and a total cannot. This page is not legal advice on trade marks or design rights.
  • Where you sell. A colour or number that is welcome in one community may read differently in another, so check the places your customers are.

A mark you already have, or two you are choosing between

A business often has a mark in use before anyone asks what the numbers say. Narayani can read it as it stands: its letter count, parts, colours and direction against the name’s number and yours, showing which points agree, which differ and which are simply absent. Closing a gap can mean a reprint, a repaint or a new sign, so whether a gap is worth closing is a business decision.

The same reading can be run on two or three versions a designer has offered. Sometimes the numbers separate them. Often they do not, and then they have nothing to add and the choice is yours. A reading that finds nothing to add is a fair result.

When the mark is carrying a question

A mark that has been redrawn several times in a short while is sometimes standing in for a question that no mark can answer: what the business is for, who it serves, or whether the partners agree. Narayani listens for that before she opens a table of colours.

Then she asks what you want a stranger to understand in two seconds, which is a question a designer would ask too. The numbers answer a smaller one: whether the mark contains what you want it to contain. If a colour was ruled out because someone called it unlucky, she asks who said so and what the list was based on.

What is known about numbers in a logo

No study shows that the numbers in a logo, the colours a tradition links with a number or the direction of a mark’s lines change how a business does. The general evidence on numerology is on the main numerology page, and the evidence on signature reading is on the signature page.

Colour and number meanings are conventions, and they differ between lists, communities and countries. Narayani presents them as that. No result is promised, and a good mark does not stand in for a good business.

The mark, the name and the brief to bring

  • The name, spelled exactly as it is meant to appear
  • Your date of birth, so that your own numbers can be read beside the name’s
  • The existing mark, or the versions you are weighing, as an image if you have one
  • Where the mark will appear: a sign, packaging, a vehicle, a phone screen
  • Any brief your designer has given you, or questions they have asked

Questions people ask

If yours is not here, ask it when you enquire.

Ask something else
Can you give me a finished logo?

No logo design or production service is promised here. Ask about the available guidance, and keep design decisions with your chosen designer.

Should a logo change after a name change?

The wordmark has to, because it carries the name. The rest need not. Letter count and initials change with the name, while the shapes and colours can stay if they still suit the business. Redrawing everything asks customers to learn a new name and a new look together, which is a real cost. Narayani can read the new name against the old mark and show where they agree and differ. Whether to redraw is a business decision.

Can a number be hidden in a logo?

Yes, as a count: five leaves, eight stripes, three bars. A designer can build the count in so that nobody notices it, and a numeral can be drawn from shapes, such as an 8 made of two rings. Nothing shows that a hidden number does anything for the business. If it pleases you to know it is there, that is a fair reason. It should never cost the mark its legibility.

Do I have to change my logo if it does not match my numbers?

No. A mismatch is a difference between a mark and a list, and the lists disagree with one another. Narayani shows which points differ and what closing each would cost, from a reprint to a new sign. Nothing in the tradition requires a change, and no mismatch is a reason to worry about the mark you have. Bring an image of it if you have one.

How many colours or parts should a logo have?

There is no rule. Some practitioners align the count of colours or parts with a number from the name or the owner, and the lists disagree about which numbers are favourable. Practical limits are firmer: fewer colours print more reliably and cost less to reproduce, and fewer parts stay readable at small sizes. If a certain count means something to you, the designer can use it where it does no harm.

Will the right logo bring luck or more customers?

Nothing shows that it does. Claims for a lucky logo rest on lists and conventions, and none has been tested. A mark helps in ordinary ways: customers recognise it, remember the business and notice the care that went into it, and those come from the design and the business behind it. The numbers can give you one more reason for a choice you were already making.

What if my designer does not want to work with numerology?

That is a fair position, and the guidance allows for it. Everything comes as an option with its reason, and the designer decides which options to use. Legibility, reproduction and distinctiveness come first, and a designer who turns down a count or a colour on those grounds is doing the job. If the designer turns down all of it, you still have a mark you chose for your own reasons.

Which numbers does a logo follow: the business name’s or my own?

The name’s number is the usual starting point, because the mark carries the name. Your own numbers are read beside it. They often differ, and when they do the guidance shows both and leaves the choice to you: a count from the name, say, and a colour family from yours. Where both cannot be honoured, the designer’s judgement settles the design and you decide which number to favour.
