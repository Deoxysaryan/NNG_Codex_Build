Rudraksha beads, mukhi and malas

When you want to know what a mukhi means, which bead is real, and how a mala is worn.

Read the background: the introduction

A Rudraksha bead is a seed that people have strung and prayed with for a long time. It is plain and grooved, and it carries a great many claims. Some come from the texts, some from sellers, and some from neither.

This is background reading from published sources, and the page keeps the three apart. It says what the two main texts say about each bead, what modern sellers add, where botany and the trade disagree, and how to check that a bead is what it is called. It also says plainly what has and has not been tested.

What she looks at when a bead comes up

  • What is prompting the question: a family custom, a wish to pray with a mala, or a warning someone gave you
  • Where the advice came from: a text, a seller’s table or a practitioner, and whether it gives its reasons
  • What you have already bought or been shown, and what paper came with it
  • Your chart and the period running, where the question is about timing
  • Whether a plain mala for japa is all you need

What a Rudraksha is, and where the tree grows

Rudraksha is a seed bead used in personal and prayer practice. Its natural grooves distinguish it from the polished mineral stones in the collection.

Nepal, Indonesia or India: what sellers say, and what is unsettled

Origin labels and seller grades are not a universal quality standard. Check the individual bead and the supporting evidence, not the country label alone.

What mukhi means, and where the counts come from

Mukhi means face: the count of natural grooves on the bead. Traditional meanings vary, and the count does not establish a medical benefit.

Malas: 108 beads and one more

A traditional mala has 108 counting beads and a separate guru bead. Customs guide how it is used; they are not guarantees of an outcome.

Wearing and caring for a bead: what the texts say, what sellers add

The Shiva Purana says a Rudraksha should be worn with a mantra, and that beads which are worm-eaten, unpierced or damaged should be avoided.

Telling a real bead from a copy

Appearance alone cannot settle authenticity. Ask about an independent inspection or imaging and confirm buying terms. Floating a bead in water is not reliable proof.

Who may wear it, and what is claimed for it

Wearing a bead is a personal choice, not protection against a predicted danger. Do not replace medical care or practical decisions with benefit claims.

No study found shows that wearing a bead has a medical use. Take any symptom to a doctor, and ask a seller who attaches a health claim to a bead for the evidence behind it.

Fourteen beads, from one face to fourteen. Each card gives the deities the two texts name, the planet readings sellers add, and a fact about the bead itself. What a Rudraksha is, and where the tree grows

Rudraksha is the hard stone inside the fruit of Elaeocarpus trees, called Elaeocarpus ganitrus in the trade. The tree is an evergreen with blue fruit about 1.5 to 2.3 centimetres across. The stone inside carries natural grooves running from end to end, and internal chambers that botanists call locules, usually counted at four to seven depending on the source. Elaeocarpus trees grow from north-east India and the Himalayan foothills through South-East Asia to northern Australia and the Pacific.

The name means Rudra’s eye. According to the Rudrakshajabala Upanishad, the beads were born from the eyes of Rudra, another name for Shiva, and a popular version of the legend speaks of tears. That is tradition, and for many people the bead is a devotional object before anything else.

Even the tree’s name depends on whom you ask. The trade and many Indian papers use Elaeocarpus ganitrus, while plant databases list the tree as E. angustifolius or E. sphaericus. A seller who states one correct botanical name is simplifying.

Nepal, Indonesia or India: what sellers say, and what is unsettled

Sellers sort beads mainly by where they grew. The ranges below are the sellers’ own, they differ from one seller to the next, and none is an official grade.

NepalLarger, 14 to 40 mm; a five-mukhi bead 18 to 28 mmDeep groovesThe Arun valley is named as a source
IndonesiaSmaller, 5 to 12 mm in one seller’s range and up to 25 mm in others; a five-mukhi bead about 7 to 10 mmFine groovesJava and Sumatra are named as sources
IndiaNo agreed rangeNo agreed descriptionAssam and Haridwar are named by one seller, Kulu and Rameswaram by a reference work

Sellers say Nepali and Indonesian beads are one species, and one reference work suggests that the small stones used in classic malas may come from a special cultivation technique or from an unknown race. Sellers also take opposite sides on which is better. One prices large Nepali beads at a premium. Another argues that Java beads and small beads are as good as large ones. Neither side has evidence of an effect to offer.

The sizes themselves raise a question. Botanical references give the fruit as 1.5 to 2.3 centimetres across, so the largest sizes sellers quote do not sit easily with them, and the point is unresolved. Scripture does not settle it either. The Shiva Purana grades beads by size, from the size of an amla fruit, the Indian gooseberry, down to a gram seed, and elsewhere praises small beads. Size and origin are fair reasons to prefer a bead for how it looks and feels, and they say nothing about what it does.

What mukhi means, and where the counts come from

Mukhi means face. Each bead carries natural grooves from one end to the other, and the count of grooves is its mukhi. Botanists describe the faces as matching the chambers inside the seed, normally five, and regional studies report from one to fourteen faces, with most beads showing five or six. Sellers list beads of fifteen to twenty-one faces. No academic source for those counts has been found, and botanical references give four to seven chambers, so treat them as unverified.

Two texts name a deity for each bead: chapter 25 of the Shiva Purana and the Rudrakshajabala Upanishad. Neither names a planet for any bead. Planets, Moon signs and chakras come from modern sellers, and the sellers disagree. The catalogue on this page gives each bead’s deities from both texts and shows the planet readings side by side wherever sellers differ.

Malas: 108 beads and one more

A mala is a string of 108 counting beads plus one larger bead, called the meru, sumeru, bindu or guru bead, which is not counted. When your fingers reach it, you turn the mala around rather than cross it. Malas of 27 and one, and of 54 and one, also exist, and the reasons given for 108 vary from one source to the next. The Shiva Purana chapter mentions strings of several lengths, 108 among them.

A mala is used for japa, the repeated saying of a mantra, and japa malas often use small beads of around 5 millimetres. The counting rule is a custom. It keeps the count steady so that attention can stay with the words, and a mala of any count does that.

Wearing and caring for a bead: what the texts say, what sellers add

The Shiva Purana says a Rudraksha should be worn with a mantra, and that beads which are worm-eaten, unpierced or damaged should be avoided. The texts also ask the wearer to abstain from meat, garlic, onion and liquor, which one modern seller calls optional. Everything else comes from sellers and families, and it differs.

None of these customs has evidence behind it, and none is a test of devotion. Pick the few that fit your own week.

Telling a real bead from a copy

A genuine bead has grooves that run unbroken from one end to the other, a rough natural texture, small irregularities and no carved symbols. Sellers rate the usual checks as follows.

CheckWhat it can showHow far to rely on it
Grooves, by eye or with a loupeWhether the grooves are unbroken and natural, and whether the surface looks carvedA fair first look, and better with a trained eye
X-ray or CT imageWhether the inner chambers match the number of groovesRated highest by sellers, who also say it can mislead without an expert reading, and an under-developed seed may not show well
Water testLittle. Dry beads may float, oiled ones sink, and weighted copies sinkNot reliable. All four sellers say it is not conclusive, though one still takes sinking as the usual sign
Milk, copper coin and flame testsVery littleMisleading or weak, and a flame can damage the bead

Copies you will meet

Carved grooves
Extra grooves cut into a bead to raise its mukhi count. Natural grooves run unbroken from end to end and line up with the seed’s chambers, which is what an X-ray can show.
Glued joined beads
Gauri Shankar is the name for two beads naturally joined, and trijuti for three. Sellers warn that glued pairs and triples are common, so look closely at the seam first.
Bhadraksha as a one-mukhi
A half-moon bead called kaju dana, or bhadraksha, is sold as a one-mukhi. One seller calls it a different seed and a round one-mukhi a myth, so ask exactly what is being sold.

Sellers also report other seeds and carved wood passed off as Rudraksha, and Indonesian beads sold as Nepali ones.

Who may wear it, and what is claimed for it

The Shiva Purana opens wearing to any class, stage of life or sex, and modern guides add conditions that differ. None of the guides read for this page gives a rule for children, and small beads can choke, so a young child is better without one. A bead is a devotional object. Nobody has to wear one, and no evidence shows that a person is worse off without.

Claims for health call for the most care. The table sets what exists beside what is claimed.

Questions people ask

If yours is not here, ask it when you enquire.

Ask something else
Which mukhi is right for me?

Neither main text names a bead for a person. The Shiva Purana and the Upanishad link each bead with a deity. Planets, Moon signs and needs such as protection or wealth are added by modern guides, which choose by a full chart, by the Moon sign or by present needs, and disagree with one another. So ask any adviser for the reasoning. Liking a bead, and wanting to pray with it, is also a reason.

Can women wear Rudraksha?

The Shiva Purana puts no bar on sex, so the text allows it. Guides of recent date differ on menstruation: some set conditions and some set none, which leaves the matter to a household’s custom. If someone says a woman must not wear a bead, you can reasonably ask which text says so. Whether to wear one is a devotional decision for the person wearing it.

How do I wear a Rudraksha, and is there a day to start?

The Shiva Purana says to wear it with a mantra, and the guides add a thread, often red, or a chain. One guide names Monday for the first wearing and others name different days. No rule is common to all, and no evidence shows that the day, the thread or the mantra changes anything. Choose a way you will keep up without fuss, and begin on a day that suits your own routine.

Is a laboratory certificate for a Rudraksha trustworthy?

No public standard for Rudraksha reports, comparable to the one gem laboratories follow, was found. One seller calls its partner laboratory government-authorised, and another warns that many laboratories are unverified. Read what the paper shows: an image, a chamber count, the laboratory’s name, an identifying number and a date. Count it as the seller’s own evidence, and ask whether the image on it is of the bead in your hand.

More questions (7)
Does a Rudraksha need to be energised before it is worn?

Energising is a ritual some sellers add, with mantras and offerings. No effect of it has been measured, and a label that says a bead is energised cannot be checked, so it is no reason to pay more. If a rite means something to you, the Shiva Purana mentions a mantra said when the bead is worn, and that is a practice you can follow yourself without paying extra.

Can I wear several mukhis on one thread?

Combined sets are on sale, and several guides recommend wearing two or more mukhis together, but the pairings differ from one guide to the next and no test of any of them was found. Starting with a single bead, and seeing whether you keep wearing it, is a gentler first step. A string of many different faces is largely a question of taste and cost.

Do I take it off to sleep or bathe, and how do I care for it?

Guides disagree, so there is no single rule to follow. A seed can dry out and crack, which is the reason guides give for a drop of light oil now and then, and soap, perfume and chemicals are the usual things to keep away from it. Whether to sleep in it is a matter of comfort and custom, and a broken thread simply needs restringing.

Is a larger Nepali bead better than a small Indonesian one?

No evidence says so. The two sellers’ claims are opposite, and neither is backed by a measured effect, as the section on origins shows. What does differ is practical: size, the depth of the grooves and how comfortably a bead sits in a mala. Choose on those, on your budget and on the paperwork the seller can offer. A higher price for a larger bead is a price for size.

Why do some mukhis cost more than others?

Price follows size, rarity and what the seller can show about the bead. It does not follow any effect, because none has been shown. Counts above six are unusual, since regional studies report most beads at five or six faces, and unusual counts cost more. A high price is no evidence that a bead is real, and a very low one is a reason to ask what it is. This page gives no prices; where one applies, it is shared when you enquire.

Which Rudraksha is suggested during Sade Sati or a hard period?

Some sellers tie a mukhi to Saturn or to another planet, and so to a period, but neither main text makes any such tie and the sellers disagree with each other. Sade Sati is the roughly seven-and-a-half-year period when Saturn passes the signs around the Moon sign, and the dashas and transits page has the detail. No study found shows that a bead changes a period. In the order she keeps, a bead, if any, comes last.

Is a Rudraksha linked with a chakra, a nakshatra or a Moon sign?

Sellers publish tables that pair beads with Moon signs, nakshatras and chakras. None of these pairings is in the two main texts, the tables differ between sellers, and no test of these pairings turned up. Chakras come from yoga and tantra, and they are not a medical concept. A table that gives one bead per Moon sign is one seller’s scheme and says nothing about your own chart.
